# What Arrived Without Its Head

São Luís, late — the night he told her

The news came the way all news came to São Luís, which was late, secondhand, and by way of somebody who had been told it by somebody on a ship.

A Dutch trader had it from a factor in Belém, who had it from Lisbon, and by the time it reached the tamarind it had been through four mouths and two languages and had acquired the shape that such stories always acquire, which is the shape of a joke.

They had dug up Descartes.

He had died in Stockholm sixteen years ago, in the service of the Queen of Sweden, and France had finally decided it wanted him back. They had put him in a box and started him across Europe, and the box had been eight months on the road.

And when they opened it in Paris, the head was gone.

---

Fermat did not laugh, which the trader plainly found disappointing.

He asked three questions instead, in a voice that he heard, from somewhere outside himself, doing the courtroom thing.

Was it certain the skull was absent. It was.

Was anything else absent. Yes — the right index finger, which was not a mystery at all: the French ambassador to Sweden had taken it, quite openly, as a personal souvenir.

And what were people saying.

The trader shrugged. People were saying what people say. That the Swedes had kept the head on purpose. That a chemist had it. That someone had sold it. That the ambassador had been the least of the thieves. And — the trader said this last as though it were the funniest part, and drank — *that it was not him at all. That the box that left Stockholm was not the box that arrived, and that the great philosopher, who had been so very clever about everything else, had been clever about this too.*

---

He walked home the long way, past the fort, and did not go in for some while.

Marie found him on the step with his hands on his knees.

"You have the face," she said.

"I have the face."

"Which face."

"The one from the corridor at the fort." He did not look up. "Sit down. I have to tell you something, and I have been arranging it in my head for two hours and it has not improved."

---

He gave it to her in order, because that was the only way he could give anything.

"A body has crossed Europe this year and arrived incomplete," he said, "and the whole of Paris is now discussing whether the man in the coffin is the man on the plate. That is the first thing."

"And?"

"And they are *enjoying* it, Marie. That is the second thing, and it is the one that matters. It is not an accusation. Nobody has been charged with anything. It is a diversion — a thing men say at a table, about a philosopher, in a year when there is not much else. It costs them nothing." He turned his hands over. "But it puts the *shape* into the world. The shape of the question. That a coffin is a claim, and a claim can be wrong, and that a clever man might have made it wrong on purpose."

"You think someone will apply the shape to you."

"I think a shape, once it exists, gets applied to everything that fits it. It is the only reliable law I know about people." He rubbed his face. "And I fit it perfectly. I was clever. I died at a convenient distance. There was no grave anyone can open — I made certain of that, and it was the best decision in the whole design, and it is now the single fact about my death that a man at a table would find most interesting."

---

She let that sit.

"There is a third thing," she said. "You would not have this face for two of them."

"There is a third thing."

He was quiet long enough that she put her hand on his arm.

"Clément-Samuel has my offices," he said. "He took them in the year I died — that was arranged, it was proper, it is what a son does. And he has my papers. All of them. Everything in the house, everything in the drawer, forty years of it."

"Yes."

"He will publish them."

He said it flatly and then stopped, and Marie waited, and after a while he went on in a voice she had heard perhaps twice.

"He was the one who understood what I was. The others loved me. Clément-Samuel *read* me. He used to come in and sit and not speak until I looked up, and then ask the one question I had not wanted asked." Fermat's mouth moved. "He will spend years on it. He will go through every scrap and letter, and he will get it right, because he is careful and because he will think he is doing the only thing left that he can do for me."

"Pierre."

"And when he is finished he will print, among a great many other things, a note I made in the margin of a Diophantus about twenty-five years ago, in which I state that I have a demonstration of a certain proposition and that the margin is too narrow to contain it."

He looked up at her for the first time.

"And every mathematician in Europe will read it. And they will want to *ask me.*"

---

"They cannot ask you," Marie said. "That is the entire point of what we did."

"They cannot ask me. So they will look for the proof. And when they cannot find the proof, some of them — not many, but the field does not need many — will begin to wonder whether a man who left that particular note behind was quite as finished as everyone assumed." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I am not saying it is likely. I am saying it is *the same shape.* A dead man who is unusually clever, and something missing from the record, and no grave to settle it."

"You are frightening yourself with a coincidence."

"I am frightening myself with a *class* of coincidence, which is a different animal and I have spent my life on it." He almost smiled. "But you are right that I have no business doing it out loud, so let me say the part I actually came home to say, which I have been walking around for two hours."

---

"My son is going to spend five years of his life being a good son to a man who is sitting on a step in Maranhão."

Marie did not say anything at all.

"He will be forty when he finishes. He will have given the best working years he has to assembling his father, and he will do it beautifully, and he will believe every day of it that he is completing something. And the whole enterprise is a kindness performed on a lie that I told him." He was not crying; he simply had the flat voice. "And the terrible part — the part I have not been able to put down since the trader stopped talking — is that I want him to do it. I want it very badly. I have wanted it since La Rochelle and I have not once admitted it."

"Because it is your work."

"Because it is my work, and because it is the only version of me that gets to survive, and because I am *vain*, Marie, in the one way I could never train out of myself." He put his head in his hands. "I let them bury a carter. I have not sent word in eighteen months. And what I am sitting here grieving is not my wife and not my children. It is that I will never know whether the boy got the Diophantus right."

---

She did not tell him he was a good man. She had never once done that, and he had loved her for eleven years partly on that basis.

She said: "Then he gets to finish it."

"What?"

"He gets to finish it. That is the thing you are describing, and you are describing it as a horror." She turned to face him properly. "Pierre, you cannot write to him. If you write to him you kill him, and Duval, and Agnès, and probably me. There is no version where you send that letter. So the only question left is what you do with the wanting, and I am telling you what to do with it."

"Which is?"

"You let it be a gift instead of a theft." Her hand was still on his arm. "He will publish his father, and he will be proud, and he will be right to be — because everything he prints will be *true*, every line of it. You did the work. The only false thing in the entire volume will be the date on the front."

Fermat sat with that for a long moment.

"That is a magistrate's argument," he said eventually. "That is a very good magistrate's argument."

"I sat next to you at a table in Toulouse for six years listening to you make them. I did not waste the time."

---

They stayed out there a while longer, in the dark, and the buffalo came up the north road at the hour she always came and stood at the edge of the light doing nothing whatsoever, and neither of them remarked on it, because by then it would have been strange if she had not.

"The margin," Fermat said at last.

"Mm."

"I have been telling people for forty years that there was not room. It was the most useful sentence I ever wrote. It was not even a lie — there genuinely was not room, on that page, that day." He shook his head slowly. "But there was never room in Toulouse either, and I let one stand for the other, and now a boy who cannot read is going to know the whole of it and my son is going to publish the excuse."

"Does that trouble you?"

He thought about it honestly, which took a while.

"No," he said, and sounded surprised. "That is the strangest part. It does not. If the choice is that Europe keeps the sentence and Maranhão keeps the proof — I find I would make that trade again. Twice." He stood up, slowly, sixty-five years old and stiff in every joint the sea had ruined. "It is the only thing I have arranged in two years that I am not ashamed of."
