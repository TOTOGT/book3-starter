# The Man Who Could Not Be Interesting

São Luís, the second month

His name was Duarte Nunes, and he sold cloth, and he was the most careful man Fermat had ever met — which was saying a considerable amount, from a magistrate who had spent thirty years watching men be careful in front of him for a living.

It took Fermat eleven days to work out why.

---

It was not one thing. It was the accumulation, and Fermat had spent his life on accumulations.

The household did no work on Saturdays, and had an explanation ready — a good one, worn smooth, offered a half-second faster than a true explanation is usually offered. There was no pork in that house, and there was a reason for that too. The old woman lit two lamps on Friday evening and put them in an inner room where the street could not see them, and the shutters on that side were closed before the sun went, every week, in a town where nobody closed shutters against anything but rain.

And when a Portuguese official came down that street — not to their door, merely down that street — the entire family became, for the length of his passage, *slightly less present.* Nothing so crude as hiding. Fermat watched it happen twice and could not afterward describe what had changed, only that a house which had been occupied was now simply a house.

He had seen this before. Not here. In Toulouse, in a courtroom, in the particular quality of a witness who has rehearsed being ordinary.

---

He said nothing for eleven days, and then said the wrong thing on purpose, in the way of a man who has decided he would rather know.

They were talking about Recife. Duarte had mentioned it once, as a place he had lived, in a sentence engineered to be uninteresting.

"Fifty-four," Fermat said. "When the Portuguese took it back."

"Yes."

"A great many people left."

"A great many people left," Duarte agreed, and did not elaborate, and folded a length of cloth that did not need folding.

"I understand," Fermat said, "that some of them went to Amsterdam. And some, I am told, went north on very short notice." He kept his eyes on the cloth. "And I understand that what arrived in Recife behind the Portuguese flag was not only the Portuguese flag."

The folding stopped.

It was, Fermat thought afterward, the longest silence he had ever been responsible for, and he had once told a man he was to be hanged.

"You are a magistrate," Duarte said. Not a question. "You did not tell anyone. But you are."

"I was."

"Toulouse."

"How—"

"You correct people's *grammar*, senhor." Duarte's voice had gone very quiet and very flat. "You cannot stop. And you did it in Latin, twice, to a priest. Nobody in this town has any idea what that means. I know exactly what it means."

They looked at each other for a while.

"I have not been a magistrate since January of last year," Fermat said. "I have not been anything since January of last year. Whatever you are afraid I am — I am four days newer at not being it than you are."

---

He learned the shape of it slowly, over weeks, mostly from things nobody said.

There were four households. They had come up from Pernambuco in the winter of fifty-four, separately, by different routes, which was itself a decision somebody had made carefully. They had been in Recife when there was a synagogue on the Street of the Jews and a man could say what he was out loud in the Americas for the first time in a hundred and fifty years, and they had been in Recife when that ended.

Duarte was the one who knew the prayers.

That was the entire office. There was no congregation, no building, no title — nobody called him anything, because a word said aloud is a word that can be repeated. He was a man who sold cloth and who happened to have memorised what his grandfather memorised, and once or twice a year, in an inner room with the shutters shut, he was something else for an hour, and then he was a cloth merchant again by morning.

He had two names, and only one of them had ever been written down.

*Three of us*, Fermat thought. *Pedro. Maria. Duarte.* And underneath each of them a name that existed only in a room with the door closed.

---

The difficulty arrived in the second month, and it arrived, as difficulties do, in the form of ordinary kindness.

Senhora Ferreira, who was fifty and widowed and ran the best household on that street and was — like most of the Portuguese women of São Luís, whose grandmothers had been Tupinambá and whose grandfathers had come off ships — a woman with an entirely accurate sense of who in this town was worth anything, had begun sending things to the Nunes house.

Nothing improper. A dish. A question about cloth that did not require an answer. Her daughter, twice.

And Senhora Ferreira had opinions, and shared them, warmly, in the street, at volume: that Duarte Nunes was the only man in São Luís who listened to the end of a sentence. That he did not drink. That he had beautiful hands and a beautiful, sad, patient face and had plainly buried a wife somewhere and never spoke of it. That a man like that should not be alone.

Three other women had agreed with her out loud within Fermat's hearing in a single week.

---

Fermat went to see him about it, and found him grey.

"You have noticed," Duarte said.

"I have noticed that half this street has decided you are the most interesting man in it."

"Yes." He was not folding anything. That was how Fermat knew. "Senhor — how long have you been a magistrate?"

"Thirty years."

"Then tell me. In thirty years. How does it *start?*"

Fermat did not answer at once, because he knew the answer and did not want to say it in this room.

"A denunciation," he said.

"Yes."

"And a denunciation requires that somebody has been *thinking about you*." He heard his own voice doing the flat courtroom thing and could not stop it. "It is never the evidence first. The evidence is found afterward, by men who have been told where to look. What comes first is that a person has become notable. And once a person is notable, every ordinary thing about him acquires a second meaning, and the shutters closed on a Friday evening stop being shutters."

"Say the rest."

"A woman is refused," Fermat said. "Or her daughter is. Or a man watches his wife praise you in the street for a year. And nothing happens for a long time, and then one day something else goes wrong in that house — a debt, a death, a bad harvest — and there is a thing they have been holding, and it is free to say, and it costs them nothing."

Duarte nodded slowly, as though a physician had confirmed a diagnosis he had made himself months ago.

"In Portugal," he said, "the accusation is not *he is a Jew.* Nobody has to prove that. The accusation is about the women. It has always been about the women. My grandfather could have recited you the phrasing."

---

"Then refuse them," Fermat said, knowing as he said it that it was wrong.

"And be a man who refuses a respectable widow in front of her friends." Duarte smiled without any part of his face enjoying it. "Senhor, you do not understand the position. I cannot accept, because a marriage requires a church and a church requires a priest and a priest requires a lifetime of Sundays performed correctly under a man trained to watch for exactly what I am. And I cannot refuse, because a refused woman remembers. And I cannot leave, because we have already left, twice, and there is nowhere further north that is not the same country."

He picked up the cloth again, finally, because his hands needed it.

"So I must be pleasant. Every day. For years. And never once interesting."

---

Fermat walked home the long way, past the fort with the French king's name on it, thinking about the arithmetic of it — because that was the only thing he had ever been able to do with something that hurt.

He had spent his life proving impossibilities. He knew what a genuinely impossible system looked like: not one that is hard, but one where the constraints admit no solution at all. *Do not accept. Do not refuse. Do not leave. Do not be dull enough to insult them. Do not be interesting enough to be remembered.*

There was no point in that space. He checked it twice, walking, the way he checked everything, and it held.

And the cruelty of it — the part that stayed with him for years, that he thought about on the north road and on Marajó and at the end — was that the trap had been sprung by nothing but ordinary human warmth. Nobody in this had done a single wrong thing. Senhora Ferreira had lost a husband and met a kind man and said so to her friends, which is the most harmless act available to a person. Her daughter had come by twice.

*The machine does not need anyone to be cruel*, Fermat thought. *It only needs someone to be noticed, and it waits. It has all the time in the world. It is the patience of it.*

He had built his own disappearance out of a plague and a carter's body and three days between a death and a departure in one port, and had been rather proud of it.

He understood, walking up that street, that he had solved a problem Duarte Nunes had been living inside since he was born — and that the only reason his own solution had worked was that he had been given something no *converso* in the Americas had ever been given, which was somewhere else to go, and a body to leave behind that was not his own.
