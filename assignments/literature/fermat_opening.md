# The Margin

Toulouse, 1653

The plague had a sense of humor, Fermat decided, somewhere around the third day of the fever. It had chosen him — a man who spent his life proving things could *not* exist, no solutions, no integers, no possible triangle — and was now attempting to prove, with rather more force than his own arguments ever mustered, that he did not exist either.

He found this, on the whole, very funny.

"You are laughing," said his manservant, alarmed, dabbing his forehead with a cloth that had long since given up being cool.

"I am appreciating an irony," Fermat said. "Go and tell them I'm dead. Then come back in a week and tell them you were wrong."

He was not wrong to expect he might die, and not wrong, either, that he wouldn't — not yet. Bernard Medon wrote to a friend in the Netherlands that the great Fermat had died; a week later he wrote again, sheepishly, that the great Fermat had un-died, and sent his regards.

It was, Fermat thought afterward, the only proof he'd ever produced that people were eager to see retracted.

---

He remembered, in the worst of the fever, a conversation from some years before — half memory, half the fever's own invention, he could never afterward say which — with a king who had wanted, very badly, to understand mathematics, and had not been able to.

Not from lack of trying. The king had sat through explanations of Fermat's own devising — of numbers that refused to be the sum of two others, of a certain triangle whose sides could never all be whole — and had nodded in the particular way that powerful men nod when they have decided that understanding is beneath the effort of pretending not to understand.

"Descartes made it look easy," the king had said once, not quite an accusation.

"Descartes," Fermat had replied, "made *himself* look easy. That is a different accomplishment."

The king had laughed at that — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and for a moment Fermat had liked him.

"Teach me the thing in the margin," the king said. "The one you won't finish."

"I finished it," Fermat said. "The margin didn't."

"Then use a bigger margin."

"Your Majesty," Fermat said, "I have been offered bigger margins my whole life. I have never once taken one. I find the smallness clarifying."

The king had not understood that either. But he'd stopped asking, which Fermat took, in a man of his position, as a kind of grace.

---

He did not die of the plague in 1653. He died twelve years later, in Castres, two days after signing his last judgment for the court — still a magistrate to the end, mathematics forever the thing he did in the margins of his actual life, never the center of it, which was, he sometimes thought, the only theorem he'd ever gotten exactly backward.
