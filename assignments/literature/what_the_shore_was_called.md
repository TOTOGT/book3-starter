# What the Shore Was Called

Maranhão, 1666

The children saw them first.

Fermat was aware of the shouting some time before he was able to do anything about it — a high, delighted, entirely unafraid noise coming out of the green on the left side of the world, getting closer, splitting into several voices and then a dozen. He had his face against the animal's neck. He had had his face against the animal's neck for a number of hours that he would never afterward be able to fix, and he lifted it now with the slowness of a man operating a body by correspondence.

There were children running along the shoreline.

They came out of the mangrove at a dead sprint, five or six of them, then more behind, hurdling roots and going through the shallows in great flat sheets of spray, screaming at each other and pointing and screaming again — and one of them, a girl of perhaps nine, out ahead of the rest, turned her face up toward him as she ran.

Her eyes were blue.

Fermat looked at her for what felt like a long time and was probably not.

It was not possible. That was his first thought, arriving with the flat mechanical clarity of a man too exhausted for any other kind: *it is not possible*, in the specific sense in which he had spent forty years using the phrase — not *unlikely*, not *surprising*, but **excluded**, ruled out, a thing that does not occur in this system. He had crossed four thousand leagues. He had been five months at sea. Everything on this coast was supposed to be, in the most ordinary and unremarkable way, *not French*, and the child running toward him through the surf had the eyes of a girl from Béarn.

He was too tired to solve it. He filed it, the way he filed everything, and it sat in him like a stone for the next three days.

---

What he did not understand — could not have, not for weeks — was that he had it exactly backward.

The children were not shouting about him.

They had seen Europeans. Europeans were the least interesting thing in São Luís; the town was full of them, sweating in the wrong clothes, and there was nothing about two more, however wrecked, that would move a child to run.

**They had never seen a buffalo.**

There was no such animal on that coast. There had never been such an animal on that coast. What came out of the surf that afternoon, enormous and grey and completely unhurried, chest-deep and then knee-deep and then up onto the sand with water sheeting off a back as broad as a table — was, to every child on that shoreline, an object with no name and no category and no precedent, arriving out of the sea under its own power.

And there were people on top of it.

That was the part they were screaming about. Not the beast, and not the strangers, but the *combination* — the impossible animal, and the two half-drowned creatures riding it, coming up out of the water together like something out of a story that nobody had told them yet.

They ran alongside it the whole way in, shrieking, keeping a careful and entirely sensible six feet of distance, and the buffalo took no more notice of them than she had taken of the storm, or the reef, or the man who had bought her.

---

The animal put them down in shallow water and walked away without ceremony, up the mud and into the mangrove, and neither of them thought to thank it — which Fermat regretted for the rest of his life, not because he believed the beast would have understood, but because he had spent forty years insisting that a thing's not needing to be said was no argument against saying it.

Marie lay in six inches of warm brown water and did not move for a long time.

"Are you alive," Fermat said. It was not a question, quite. He had lost the shape of questions somewhere around the fourth hour.

"I appear to be." Her voice came out wrecked. "I had rather counted on you to have arranged otherwise."

"I arranged one death. I was not commissioned for two."

She laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that he crawled the three feet between them and put a hand on her back, and they stayed like that while the tide came in around them and the light went the particular orange of a latitude he had read about and never once expected to stand in.

---

He had brought nothing ashore.

Not the trunk, which had been beyond him from the first minute. Not the smaller bag. Not the packet of letters, not the instruments, not one coin of the second-largest fortune in the Parlement of Toulouse — which he had spent three years converting, parcel by defensible parcel, out of land and into silver and then out of silver and into the only form of wealth he had been able to think of that no customs officer at Lisbon would trouble to open.

He had bought buffalo. Three ships of them.

It had seemed to him, at the time, the single cleverest thing he had ever done outside of mathematics. A man cannot carry a fortune across an ocean; a fortune invites exactly the questions a dead man cannot afford. But a man can carry *livestock*, openly, on a commission, with a manifest and a purpose and a bored official waving it through — and livestock, unlike silver, breeds. He had stood on the quay at Lisbon and watched them craned aboard in slings, bellowing, three holds full of great patient grey animals bought against the advice of every man he consulted, and had thought: *this is the part of the enterprise that will look foolish in a ledger and sensible in a swamp.*

He had converted everything he owned into a thing that could not be stolen, could not be seized, and could not be counted.

He had not, in three years of arithmetic, considered that it could swim away.

---

He had nearly died in the hold.

That was the part he did not tell Marie for some years, and never told anyone else. The ship was going down and he had gone *back into it* — down, against the water, into a flooding dark full of floating wreckage, for a trunk he knew perfectly well he could not lift and had gone for anyway, because thirty years of careful quiet transactions were in it and he had not been able to make himself accept a number as large as *all of it*.

He got a hand on the trunk. He could not move the trunk. He tried again, with the specific stupidity of a man who has been right about everything for forty years, and the water took the last of the air out of the space above his head, and he understood — with a clarity he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe — that he was about to drown for an object.

And then the floor came up underneath him.

Not the floor. The animal had been in that hold, and it came up under him like the sea deciding something, and he was against her back and moving, and there was no decision in it that belonged to him at all. She went out through the broken side of a ship he had bought, into the storm, with a sixty-four-year-old man on her back holding nothing whatsoever.

---

He surfaced with empty hands.

That was the phrase that stayed with him, afterward, when he tried to give the thing a shape: *empty hands*. He had gone down clutching and come up holding nothing, and the astonishing part — the part he turned over for years and never fully solved — was that somewhere in the first hundred yards of that grey water, with everything he owned sinking behind him and a woman's hand closing over his wrist beside him, he had begun, helplessly and inappropriately, to feel **light**.

Sixty-four years old, four thousand leagues from home, officially dead, comprehensively ruined.

He had never in his life felt so free.

He found this funny. He was, he recognised, in the condition where he would find most things funny for a day or so, and then would not find anything funny for rather longer. But it was not only that. He checked it twice, the way he checked a result he distrusted, and it held: with nothing left to protect, there was nothing left to be careful about, and he had been careful since he was nineteen.

"There'll be a settlement," Marie said, upright now, wringing river out of the clerk's shirt she had been wearing since Bordeaux and had stopped bothering to be a clerk inside of. "There's smoke."

"There is."

"You don't sound pleased."

"I am considering," Fermat said, "that I have spent nine months making myself into a man who does not exist, and that I am about to walk into the first place in the world with a reason to ask."

He had not, at that point, understood that the decision was no longer his.

---

They did not walk into São Luís. They were *conducted* into it.

The children would not go home. That was the first thing. They had established, by degrees and with a good deal of argument among themselves, that the two creatures from the sea were not dangerous, and having established it they lost all remaining caution and simply attached themselves — walking backward in front of Fermat so as not to miss anything, tugging at Marie's ruined sleeve, conducting a loud and unbroken commentary in a language he could not follow at a volume that made following it unnecessary.

And they had gone for the others.

By the time the track widened into something a cart had used, there were perhaps thirty. By the time the first roofs showed there were more than he could count without turning his head, and Fermat — who had spent his entire adult life in rooms where the number of people present was a matter of protocol — gave up trying.

They were of every possible age, and they were all, without exception, behaving like children.

That was what he could not place. There was a woman near the front who could not have been younger than fifty, grey coming in at her temples, who was walking backward as competently as the nine-year-old beside her and laughing at something the child had said with her whole face, entirely unembarrassed, in a way no woman of fifty in Toulouse had laughed in his presence in forty years. There was a man of perhaps sixty with a bad leg keeping pace at the edge of the crowd and shouting corrections at the tellers of the story, and being shouted down, and enjoying it.

*Nobody here is pretending to be older than they are*, Fermat thought, and did not yet know what to do with the observation, and so kept walking.

---

They were telling the story. He worked that much out.

The children at the front were performing it for the ones who had arrived late, over and over, with escalating physical commitment — one of them doing the sea with both arms, another going down on all fours to be the animal, a third climbing onto the second's back to be the riders and being thrown off, to enormous satisfaction, at every retelling.

And every new arrival looked past the performance, and past Fermat and Marie entirely, toward the water — for the animal.

Which was not there.

She had gone into the mangrove and had not come out, and Fermat understood, somewhere in the second half of that walk, that the crowd growing around him was not for him at all. It was a procession for something that had already left. Whatever the town saw that afternoon — and they would be talking about it for a very long time, in a way that had nothing to do with two exhausted Europeans — was an absence with thirty children shouting around it.

*It has been ashore four minutes*, he thought, *and it is already the thing that got away.*

He did not know, yet, how permanently true that would turn out to be, or that it was the only part of his own arrival that would outlive him.

---

Marie had the presence of mind to ask.

She caught the arm of the girl who had been out ahead on the shoreline — the one with the eyes — and pointed at the roofs ahead, and made a face that meant *what is this*, and the girl told her, twice, patiently, the second time slower.

Marie turned to him with an expression he had not seen since Versailles.

"Pierre."

"What."

"Say it back to me." And then, when he only looked at her: "*São Luís.*"

He stopped so abruptly in the track that a boy walked into the back of him.

"What."

"Say it again," he said to the boy, in bad Portuguese, and the boy said it again with the patience children reserve for the obviously unwell, and Fermat stood in the heat and did the arithmetic he had been doing all his life, which was the arithmetic of what a name is evidence of.

*Saint Louis.* Not Luís of Portugal. There was no Luís of Portugal. A city on a Portuguese coast, in Portuguese hands, taking its name from a French king — because Frenchmen had built the fort and named it for the boy on the throne at home, and had held it for three years, and had lost it, and had gone, and the name had simply stayed where they left it, the way a name will, long after everyone who meant anything by it has sailed away or been buried under it.

"Fifty-one years," he said.

"What is?"

"The French were here. They founded it. They were thrown out in the year fifteen." He was aware he sounded strange. "The Portuguese took the city and kept the name. They have been calling it after our king for half a century without, so far as I can tell, minding."

Marie looked at the low white buildings, the fort, the church, the ordinary business of a small colonial port getting on with its afternoon.

"So we've come four thousand leagues," she said, "to a French town that isn't French, that nobody bothered to rename."

"Yes."

"Pierre." She was smiling — the first one since the reef. "You could not have designed it better if you had been commissioned."

"I *was* commissioned," he said. "That is rather the difficulty."

He had it still. That was the joke, and it was not a good one. The oilcloth packet had been inside his coat since Bordeaux and the sea had not thought it worth taking: an inspection of French trading interests and remaining colonial claims in the Grão-Pará region, a modest retinue, a modest budget, the minister's own hand at the bottom. A genuine instrument of the French crown, correctly sealed, entirely in order, and made out to a man who had died in Castres in January.

He could walk into that fort and be received. He could be housed, fed, listened to, and written about in a dispatch that would reach Lisbon in four months and Paris in six.

"You could use it," Marie said, watching him not decide.

"I could use it once."

---

The commission had cost him nothing. That was the part he had never got over.

He had gone to the minister and asked for the worst post in the French imagination — a mosquito colony at the bottom of the world that no man with prospects would touch — and the minister had looked at thirty years of judgments in Toulouse and at a tired old man's joints, and had written it out on the spot, mildly puzzled and mildly relieved. Nobody investigates a magistrate of thirty years' standing who volunteers for somewhere nobody wants to go. Seniority is the only forgery that signs itself.

He had walked out of that room holding a door.

And now the door had his dead name on it.

---

They walked in through the gate at the hour when nobody guards anything, and the guard who was not guarding it glanced at two ruined Europeans and made the calculation every port official has ever made, which was that the sea had clearly already dealt with them and it was therefore not his business to.

"Names," he said anyway, because there was a book.

The packet was against Fermat's chest. He could feel the seal through the oilcloth, the small hard disc of it — the one object that had come out of the sea with him, and only because it had been sewn into a coat.

He thought, briefly, with the clarity of a man who has not slept, about what it would buy. A bed. A physician for Marie's cough. A letter of credit drawn on the French crown, which was the only remaining asset he had in the world and existed entirely because a minister at Versailles believed he was alive. Respect, which he had not wanted in France and found he wanted very much on a beach.

And a name in a Portuguese ledger, copied to Lisbon, copied to Paris, sitting in a clerk's hand somewhere in Europe next to a death notice from Castres — two entries, six months apart, in the same name, on two sides of an ocean.

One use. Then a pattern. And patterns were the one thing his entire life's work had taught him to notice in other men's mistakes.

Fermat had prepared eleven answers to this question over the preceding year. He had ranked them. He had discarded four as too elaborate, three as too dull to survive a second asking, and had settled, somewhere off the Canaries, on a merchant from Bayonne with a limp and an uninteresting reason for travelling.

He heard himself say: "Pedro."

The guard wrote it down.

"And her?"

There was a pause that Fermat would think about for years — not because it was long, it was not long, it was the length of one breath — but because in it he understood that he had never once, in all the arithmetic, worked out what she was to be called on the far side. He had solved for his own disappearance with the care of a man who knew that a single loose variable ruins a proof, and he had left her name blank, and he did not know whether that was because he had trusted her to fill it, or because some part of him had refused to write down a version of her that wasn't hers.

Marie said: "Maria."

The guard wrote that down too, with the small satisfaction of a man whose book has become tidier, and waved them through into the city that a dead king's countrymen had built and abandoned and left their king's name on.

---

That night, on a borrowed floor, with nothing under his head because there was nothing to put there, Fermat could not sleep and so did what he always did instead, which was to take a thing apart.

*The other two,* he thought.

It had no business being there. He had known that in the water and had been too busy drowning to attend to it. A buffalo is a river animal, a mud animal, an animal for shallow warm places with roots in them — not for open sea, five months out, in a storm, off a reef nobody had charted. He turned it over the way he turned over a number that refused to factor.

They had not stopped.

He had watched it happen from the water and had been too occupied with not dying to attend to what he was seeing. Two ships, standing off the reef in the grey — close enough to have put down a boat, close enough that a man aboard either of them could have counted the heads in the surf if he had wanted to — and they had held their position for some while, and then they had come about and gone.

There was no mystery in it. He worked it through anyway, because he could not stop.

They had watched him go down and had made the arithmetic every man makes: that the third ship was lost, that the commissioner was lost with it, that the cargo remaining in *their* two holds was now attached to no living owner, in an ocean where a manifest is only as true as the man standing next to it. Two shiploads of animals, a coast eight thousand miles from anyone who could ask, and a very simple sum.

They had not abandoned him out of cowardice. They had abandoned him because it paid.

*I made myself into a dead man,* Fermat thought, *and then I was surprised to be treated as one.*

---

So the herd was gone — not drowned, which he could have grieved cleanly, but *taken*, walking around inside two hulls somewhere north of him under a flag that would invent whatever story it needed. Everything he owned in the world, converted three years ago into a form that could not be stolen, stolen.

Except that some of it had got out.

He did not know how many. A hold breaks and a swimming animal does what a swimming animal does; some would have made the shore up and down that coast, some would not, and no man alive could say which or how many, and nobody would ever count them. They had come out of the water at whatever point the current put them and had walked inland into a country that was, he understood with a slowly rising and entirely inappropriate delight, *exactly what he had bought them for.* Wet ground. Standing water. A place where an ox founders.

The herd had parted ways from him, and from each other, and from any ledger that would ever exist.

*They are out there*, he thought. *All of them. And there is no document in the world that says so.*

---

And one had stayed.

That was the part that would not resolve. She had gone into the mangrove that afternoon and he had assumed, in the flat way of a man who has lost everything else, that this was simply the last of it walking off.

She came back the second evening, and stood at the edge of the settlement chewing, and looked at nothing in particular.

She did it again the evening after.

There was no explanation available that he was willing to write down. She was not tame. She was not his, in any sense a court would recognise — the papers that said so were at the bottom of the sea and made out to a dead man besides. She simply arrived, at roughly the same hour, in the manner of something that had decided a matter without consulting anyone, and stood about, and left.

It was not mercy. It had never been mercy. She had come up under him in that hold because she was swimming and he had been in the way of it, which was precisely why he had bought her in the first place.

But she kept coming back, and he did not have a word for that, and it bothered him more than the fortune did.

*A man loses the asset he counted*, he thought, *and is kept by the one he cannot.*

He had spent his life proving that certain things could not be. No three squares. No such triangle. No solution, however long you searched, however badly you wanted one. It had never troubled him. Non-existence had always seemed to him the most honest result a proof could reach, and he had built a reputation, a correspondence, and something close to a personality out of delivering it.

And now here he was, on a floor in a city named for a king he had abandoned, alive under a death certificate, having been carried out of the sea by an animal that should not have been in it, beside a woman who was supposed to have left in six weeks and left in four.

*I have become,* he thought, with the particular clarity that arrives at three in the morning to people who have had a long year, *a counterexample.*

He lay there a while longer with that.

Then, because he was who he was, he began — with no paper, no ink, and no intention of ever showing it to anyone — to look for the general case.

---

In the morning there were cattle in the street.

Not many; six or seven, moving with the total unconcern of animals that have never once been hurried, driven by a man who was not so much driving them as accompanying them in the same direction. Marie watched them pass from the doorway with her hands around a cup of something.

"They let them walk through the town."

"They let them walk everywhere, I should think." Fermat had been awake for two hours and had already learned four things about the price of hides, because he could not help it. "It is what the place is for. There is more of it inland than a man can ride across, and they say every year they push further with the herds. The city is only where the ships are."

"And what are we for?"

He did not answer at once.

One of the animals had stopped in the middle of the road for no reason available to observation and was standing there, enormous and patient, obliging the entire morning to route itself around her. Nobody shouted. A woman with a basket stepped into the gutter and back out again without breaking stride. The cow considered the street a while longer, and then, having reached whatever conclusion cows reach, moved on.

"They don't count them," Fermat said slowly.

"What?"

"There is no ledger. I have been listening all morning — they buy and sell hides by the hundred, they speak of herds inland the way you or I would speak of weather, and not one man has said a number he could defend. They cannot count them. The animals are out there, and everyone agrees they are out there, and no two men agree how many." He was aware his voice had changed. Marie had heard it change before, twice, both times at a table in Toulouse with a letter from Paris open in front of him. "Marie. They are wealthy in a thing they cannot count."

"Is that bad?"

"It is not bad. It is *unproven*." He said it the way another man might say *unclaimed*. "Do you know what I did for forty years, when the court let me? I took quantities other people were content to gesture at, and I made them exact. That is all. That is the whole of it. And I have come to the one place on God's earth that has more wealth walking loose in it than any counting-house in Europe, and no one has yet troubled to say precisely how much."

Marie set the cup down.

"You said last night you'd become a counterexample."

"I did."

"Then be a useful one," she said. "Count the cows."

---

*Note on sources, for the margin: São Luís was founded by Daniel de La Touche, Sieur de La Ravardière, in 1612, as the seat of a French colony — la France Équinoxiale. The Portuguese expelled the French at the end of 1615, after the fighting at Guaxenduba the year before. They kept the fort, the harbour, and the name. It is the only Brazilian capital founded by Frenchmen, and it has been called after Louis XIII ever since without anyone finding it necessary to stop.*

*The bull that dies and is brought back does not belong to this chapter. That festival comes later, out of the cattle country these herds were opening, and putting it in Fermat's mouth in 1666 would be a lie of the kind this book has no use for. But the cattle were already walking, and the interior was already being pushed into by men following them, and everything that Maranhão would later sing about a boi that is lost and mourned and returned was, in 1666, still only a great many animals in the dark that nobody could count.*

*Which is, as it happens, where we came in.*
