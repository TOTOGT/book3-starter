# What the Sea Already Knew

Month five, and then the reef

It was Bertrand, in the end, who gave the thing its name — not out of malice, Fermat thought later, but because sailors were a people who needed every misfortune to have a name, a shape, something that could be blamed and thereby, however uselessly, avoided next time.

"There's a woman aboard," Bertrand said to the cook, low, at the rail, in a voice that carried exactly as far as he meant it to and no farther. "Has been the whole voyage, dressed as that clerk. I'd stake my hands on it."

He did not say it as an accusation. He said it the way a man reports weather — a fact now settled into the ship's understanding of itself, passed from Bertrand to the cook to the boatswain to every hand aboard within two days, and once it had passed, it did not need to be spoken of again. It only needed to be *known*, the way sailors had always known that a woman on a ship was a debt the sea would eventually collect, in its own time, at its own price.

They did not confront Fermat. They did not confront Marie. They simply began, in the small unconscious ways sailors have always managed a feared thing, to keep their distance, to touch iron when she passed, to mutter under their breath at a wave that broke wrong or a wind that turned sour, building — plank by superstitious plank — a case against her that required no evidence at all, only the accumulating coincidence of an ordinary voyage's ordinary bad days.

By month five, when the storm came up fast and ugly off a coast none of them had charted well, there was not a man aboard who needed to be told what it meant.

---

The reef took the hull before anyone had time to do more than shout it. Fermat felt the whole ship shudder the way a body shudders at a wound it hasn't yet understood is fatal, and then the deck was tilting, and the sea was coming up through the boards in a way the sea was never meant to.

He saw the crew move before he'd fully understood they were moving *away* — not toward the rigging, not toward the pumps, not toward any of the ordinary desperate tasks of men trying to save a ship, but straight to the longboat, hauling it over with a speed and a coordination that told him, even through his own rising panic, that this had been decided before the reef, not after it. They had been waiting, without quite admitting it to themselves, for the sea to do what they'd already agreed it would eventually do.

"Rope!" he shouted, and got no answer, and understood the answer was the silence itself.

Bertrand was the last one into the boat. He looked back once — not at Fermat, and not with any triumph in it, only the flat, closed look of a man who had already decided the matter was out of his hands and put there deliberately, days ago, in his own mind, so that this moment would cost him nothing when it came.

Then the oars bit the water, and the longboat pulled away into the storm, and did not look back again.

---

It was in that moment — the ship breaking under him, the trunk of silver sliding past in the flooding hold catching what little light there was and throwing it back in a thousand small, useless brilliances, his own hand closing on the smaller bag of coin because the trunk was already gone, already too heavy, already someone else's now — that Fermat felt something enormous surface beside him in the water, and did not, at first, understand it was mercy.

The beast was vast and grey and utterly unhurried, moving through the wreck-churned water the way nothing else in that storm was moving, without panic, without hurry, as though the sea's violence were simply weather it had decided not to take personally. He grabbed for its thick, coarse hide because there was nothing else left in the world to grab for, and felt Marie's hand close over his wrist a moment later, hauling herself toward the same broad back, and neither of them, in that first drowning minute, thought to wonder why an animal built for river mud and mangrove shallows was here at all, in open water, exactly when they needed it.

They only knew that the longboat was gone, small and receding into the rain, its oars pulling away from two people the crew had already decided, weeks before the reef ever found them, deserved whatever the sea saw fit to do.

The buffalo did not deserve any of their fear. It simply swam, steady and enormous and entirely unbothered, toward a shore none of them could yet see, carrying the curse and the calculation both, equally, without troubling itself over which was which.
