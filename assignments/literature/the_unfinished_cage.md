# The Unfinished Cage

Versailles, the night she left

She had expected the dark. What she had not expected was the noise.

Even at this hour — past midnight, the court long since retired to whatever version of sleep a palace of that size permitted — the construction never fully stopped. Somewhere off toward the new wing, torches moved in a slow constellation as night crews shifted stone that the king's masons had decided could not wait for daylight. The whole western approach was a wound of scaffolding and mud, half-raised walls standing pale and skeletal against the dark, waiting for a roof that would not exist for another decade.

It was, she thought, walking fast with her case held close against her borrowed cloak, the strangest possible place to be afraid of being seen. A palace under this much construction had a thousand shadows a fleeing woman could disappear into, and a thousand workmen too exhausted, too indifferent, too used to strange lights and strange movements in the dark to look twice at one more figure hurrying somewhere it wasn't their business to ask about.

---

She had heard the story, the way everyone at court eventually did, in the particular hushed, half-mocking, half-reverent tone reserved for the things that explained a king without anyone having to say so directly: how he had been ten years old, asleep in a Paris palace, when the mob came. How they had climbed into his very bedroom to make certain he had not fled the city in the night, and how he had lain there, a child, forced to feign sleep while armed strangers loomed over him deciding, in effect, whether he still belonged to them.

He had never gone back to that city the same way again. And now, seventeen years later, he was building this — walls thick enough that no mob would ever again cross them uninvited, a court so total and so gilded that every noble in France would eventually be made to live inside it, watched, accounted for, never quite out of his sight.

Marie understood, picking her way past a stack of unfinished cornices taller than she was, that she was walking through the exact shape of his fear, made permanent in stone. He had built an entire palace so that nothing like her — nothing that could simply vanish into a night he hadn't authorized — would ever be possible again.

She was, perhaps, the last thing to slip out of Versailles before the cage was finished closing.

---

A foreman's lantern swung her way once, near the edge of the unfinished orangery, and she froze instinctively, every calculation Fermat had ever taught her running at once through her mind — *stand still, don't run, running is what gets noticed* — but the man barely glanced at her, muttered something about the night shift's water ration, and moved on. To him she was simply one more indistinct shape among the hundreds that moved through this endless, half-built site at every hour God made. The king's obsession with control had, in its earliest and clumsiest years, produced exactly the chaos a person could disappear into.

By the time she reached the tree line at the edge of the grounds, the torches behind her had shrunk to a loose, scattered scatter of light, workers still raising walls for a king who would spend the rest of his reign trying to make sure no one ever left this place again without his knowing.

She did not look back a second time. She had already seen enough: an unfinished palace, half cage and half ruin, and a king asleep somewhere inside it who did not yet know that the one thing his stone and his guards and his obsessive, wounded need for control could not do was hold onto a woman who had already decided, calmly and completely, to be gone.
