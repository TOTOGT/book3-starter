# O Conselheiro

São Luís, the first year

Nobody ever asked him for a surname.

He had prepared one. He had prepared, in the months before La Rochelle, several — a merchant family out of Bayonne, a modest lineage with a modest and entirely uninteresting history, the sort of thing that answers a question and closes it. He had rehearsed the pronunciation. He had worked out which of his own mannerisms would have to be filed off to support it.

He was never once required to produce it. Not by the corporal with the wax tablet, not by the garrison, not by the governor's secretary, not by any of the four hundred people who came to know him over the following year.

It took him a long time to understand that this was not luck. It was the local arrangement, and it was the most sophisticated thing about the town.

---

He worked it out from the direction he always worked things out from, which was the direction of the exceptions.

There were men in São Luís who were called by a trade — *the cooper*, *the pilot*, *the one who mends nets*. There were men called by where they had come from, which was never more precise than a coastline. There were men called by a thing they had once done, which in two cases he was fairly sure was a crime, cheerfully retained. There were the Le Mer, whose entire surname was a clerk's shrug at a horizon.

And then there were the ones with proper surnames, and Fermat noticed, over about four months, that those were the only names in São Luís that anybody ever said *carefully.*

Not with hostility. That was what took him so long. There was no cruelty in it that he could point to, no shunning, nothing a magistrate could have written up. Merely a small hesitation, once, when the name was first mentioned to a newcomer, and then a subject change — the sound of a town collectively agreeing not to develop a topic.

Nunes. Mendes. Cardoso. Rodrigues. Names out of Portugal that had come north with families who had come north from Pernambuco after fifty-four, and before that from Amsterdam, and before that from somewhere they did not discuss.

You could leave Recife. You could put four hundred leagues and a change of province between yourself and the Holy Office. But the name came with you — it was on the cargo manifest, on the bill of sale, on the license to trade, in the mouth of every man you had ever done business with — and it arrived before you did, and it said what it said.

*They can change what they eat*, Fermat thought. *They can change what they do on a Saturday. They can go to Mass every week of their lives and have their children go, and their children's children. They cannot change the one thing that is written on everything they will ever sign.*

He never enquired further than that. He wants it recorded, in whatever ledger receives such things, that he never once went and knocked.

---

But he understood, after that, what the town had built.

In a place where a surname could be a death sentence and a great many people's were, the whole society had quietly moved off surnames altogether. Not by decree. Nobody had decided it. It had simply become the case, over fifty years, that a man in São Luís was called by what he did, or where he'd washed up, or what he was good for — and that asking for anything more precise was a thing you did not do, because in this town the question *and your father?* had, within living memory, been the first line of an interrogation.

It was, Fermat thought, the single most humane piece of social engineering he had ever encountered, and it had been designed by nobody, and written down nowhere, and it worked.

**A surname is a claim of lineage. A title is only a claim about the present.**

That was the whole of it. A surname says: *here is where I come from, here is my grandfather, here is the thread you may pull.* A title says: *here is what I am useful for.* One of them can be followed backward by a man with a file. The other cannot be followed anywhere at all.

So the town used titles. And a great many of them, he noticed, were titles that a person had chosen the day they arrived.

---

Which was how he became *o Conselheiro*, and why it cost him nothing.

It was not conferred. Nobody granted it. It accreted, over perhaps five months, out of a series of small occasions on which a dispute over a boundary or a debt or a boat had come to a stop, and somebody had said *ask the old Frenchman, he was something once*, and he had listened to both sides all the way to the end — which, as Senhora Ferreira had observed of somebody else entirely, was a rarer skill in São Luís than anyone would credit — and had then said something short that both parties could live with.

He did it perhaps a dozen times before he noticed it had become a role. He did it fifty times before he noticed what they were calling him.

*Conselheiro.* The counsellor. The one you ask.

And the joke — which he did not share with Marie for nearly a year, because he could not work out how to say it without sounding as though he were complaining — was that it was **the same job.**

He had spent thirty years on the bench in Toulouse. He had spent three of those years, and every last sou of a fortune, and a carter's unclaimed body, and the name his father gave him, arranging never to do it again. He had crossed four thousand leagues, drowned his own money, been abandoned by his crew, been carried out of the sea by an animal, and walked into a town that could not pronounce him.

They had looked at him for five months and handed him back his own office in another language, and had not even needed to be told.

---

He kept both, in the end, and used them in different rooms, and never once got them confused.

*Pedro* was for the book. For the corporal's tablet, for the licence, for the two occasions in twenty years when something had to be signed. It was a name with the corners taken off, and it did the only work a name in a ledger is required to do, which is to be the same shape twice.

*O Conselheiro* was for the town. It was what a boy shouted from a wall. It was what a woman said at a door with a dispute behind her. It carried no father, no province, no thread to pull, and it could not be followed backward past the day he had washed up. It was, as far as he could ever determine, the safest thing anybody in São Luís possessed.

And *Pierre* was for one person, in one room, and there were years in which he heard it four or five times, and he never in his life found a way to explain to Marie what those four or five times did.

She had never had to be told either. She had simply gone on calling him the only thing he had actually brought with him — the one asset that had come through the reef, that no clerk had entered, that no Holy Office could have followed anywhere, and that was worth, he had come to understand with some precision, rather more than three ships of anything.
