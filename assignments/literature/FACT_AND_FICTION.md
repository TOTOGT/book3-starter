# Fact and Fiction — the ledger

*Pierre et Mademoiselle* · verification document · 12 August 2026

**The claim this series makes:** the world is documented; the plot is invented. Every institution, date, expulsion, ban, city, wreck and ecological outcome in this story is in the historical record. What is invented is one man's secret and one shipment of animals.

Everything below marked ✅ was verified against sources in a single afternoon. Anyone in a development meeting can repeat the exercise.

---

## ✅ Documented

### Fermat himself
- Born 1601. Magistrate of the **Parlement of Toulouse**, thirty years on the bench.
- Married **Louise de Long, 1 June 1631**. Eight children, five surviving to adulthood: Clément-Samuel, Jean, Claire, Catherine, Louise.
- **May 1653** — caught bubonic plague and survived; his health was weaker afterwards. **Bernard Medon**, *conseiller au présidial de Toulouse* and a friend, wrote to the Dutch scholar **Nicolas Heinsius the Elder** announcing Fermat's death — **and withdrew it in his next letter.** *This is the seed of the entire premise and it actually happened.*
- **Died 12 January 1665, at Castres.**
- **Clément-Samuel inherited his father's offices in 1665** and published the *Diophantus* with his father's marginal commentary — the Last Theorem — in **1670**.
- Fermat published almost nothing in his lifetime. There is live scholarship asking specifically why he stayed silent on infinitesimals; one paper is titled *"Fermat's dilemma: why did he keep mum on infinitesimals? and the European theological context."*

### The war on the infinite
- **1632** — the Society of Jesus, through its Revisors General under **Jacob Bidermann**, forbade the teaching of indivisibles in its colleges. Fermat was 31.
- **1632** — Galileo summoned to Rome. Abjured **1633**; house arrest until his death in **1642**.
- **1651** — indivisibles placed on the Society's list of permanently forbidden doctrines; four of the listed propositions bear directly on the infinitely small. Jesuit tracts against them by Guldin, Bettini and Tacquet, who wrote that the method made such war on geometry that it must itself be destroyed. Effects still felt in Jesuit mathematics a century later.

### São Luís
- **1612** — founded by **Daniel de La Touche, Sieur de La Ravardière**, as the seat of *la France Équinoxiale*, named for Louis XIII. The only Brazilian capital founded by Frenchmen.
- **1614** — the fighting at **Guaxenduba**.
- **End of 1615** — the French expelled by **Jerônimo de Albuquerque Maranhão** and **Alexandre de Moura**. The Portuguese kept the fort, the harbour, and the name. *They have never changed it.*
- **25 November 1641** — the Dutch take São Luís: nineteen ships, two thousand men, under Lichtadt and Handerson. Expelled **1644**.
- **1661, Pentecost Sunday** — the colonists of São Luís invade the Jesuit college of **Nossa Senhora da Luz** (today the Cathedral), take the priests from their cells, hold them in a private house. In **September 1661** all of them, **António Vieira** included, are put aboard the ship ***Sacramento*** and sent to Lisbon. Cause: Vieira's defence of Indians against enslavement.
- **1665–1668** — Vieira interrogated and imprisoned by the Inquisition at **Coimbra** on a charge of heresy; amnestied 1668. *He is in that cell during the events of this story.*

### The Jews of Dutch Brazil
- **1636** — **Kahal Zur Israel**, Rua dos Judeus, Recife: the first synagogue in the Americas, built by Sephardic Jews who had fled forced conversion in Iberia for the Netherlands.
- **1654** — Portugal retakes Pernambuco; the community is expelled. Some reach Amsterdam; twenty-three reach New Amsterdam and found the first Jewish community in North America; others go north. The site was only confirmed archaeologically in 2000, eight floors down, by the *mikvah*.

### Marajó, and after
- The **Marajoara** civilisation: pre-Columbian, roughly **400–1400 CE**. Mound-builders (*tesos*), polychrome funerary ceramics, hierarchical society. Occupation phases: Ananatuba, Mangueiras, Formigas, Marajoara, Aruã.
- **Bumba-meu-boi do Maranhão** — a bull that dies and is brought back. Registered by IPHAN in **2011**; inscribed by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity on **11 December 2019**.
- **The real buffalo legend:** a shipwreck around **1890** near **Cabo Maguari**, a cargo ship bound for **French Guiana** carrying water buffalo, wrecked on the sandbanks. **The surviving animals swam ashore and populated the island.** Documented importations follow in 1902 and 1906.
- **Escobar's hippos:** four brought to Colombia in the 1980s; roughly **200** in the Magdalena basin today; Colombia approved a cull of up to **80** in **April 2026** at a cost of 7.2 billion pesos, beginning in the second half of this year; the Environment Ministry projects more than 500 by 2030.

### Why a commission to Grão-Pará was available in 1665

The invented commission turns out to sit inside a real bureaucratic moment, and this is what makes it plausible rather than convenient.

- **1663** — the **Compagnie de la France équinoxiale** is *relaunched*. ⚠️ Note the name. It is the same name as the colony that founded São Luís in 1612 and lost it in 1615. France revives the enterprise, and the word, half a century later.
- **1664** — **Colbert creates the Compagnie française des Indes occidentales**, and the relaunched *France équinoxiale* company is **merged into it**. Monopoly over the sugar islands — Saint-Christophe, Guadeloupe, Martinique, and the eastern part of Saint-Domingue. Dissolved 1674.
- **June 1665** — **Bertrand d'Ogeron** is appointed by Louis XIV to govern **Tortuga and the French coast of Hispaniola**, serving 1665–68 and again 1669–75. His tenure tracks the life of the West India Company almost exactly.
- **Cayenne**, the nucleus of French Guiana, is not firmly secured until **1674**.

**What this buys the story:**

1. **The commission is motivated.** In 1665 there is a live, chartered, royally backed French enterprise whose remit is precisely the equinoctial Americas. A ministry with a new company, a new monopoly and thin information has an appetite for inspections and nobody good to send. An elderly magistrate volunteering for the worst posting available is not a suspicious anomaly — he is a solved problem.
2. **The route is real.** La Rochelle → Tortuga / French Hispaniola (brand-new governor, June 1665) → down the Guiana coast toward Cayenne → Maranhão. That is a functioning French Atlantic corridor in exactly these years, and it gives the voyage its months and its stops. It also gives the buffalo somewhere to be loaded and watered.
3. **The name closes a circle nobody has to point out.** Fermat leaves France under the authority of a revived *France équinoxiale* and washes up in the city the original *France équinoxiale* built and lost. Play it straight; the audience that catches it will feel clever, and the rest lose nothing.
4. ⚠️ **Naming collision.** Your ship's carpenter is **Bertrand**. The governor of Tortuga is **Bertrand d'Ogeron**. If the voyage touches Hispaniola, that will read as a mistake. Rename the carpenter.

*And the destination in the real Marajó buffalo legend is **French Guiana** — the same corridor, 224 years later.*

### Death as a publishing strategy — the practice this story only intensifies

This is the part that makes the premise intellectually serious rather than merely clever. **Publishing after death was a recognised workaround for dangerous ideas**, and the cleanest documented case is the one closest to Fermat.

- **Descartes.** *Le Monde* was finished and ready for publication in **1633**. On learning that Galileo had been condemned in Rome that same year — for the heliocentrism Descartes was himself defending in the book — **he withheld it.** It was published only after his death, according to his will, in **1664**. He died in 1650. So one of the two most important philosophers in Europe sat on a completed work for seventeen years because of a trial, and arranged for a corpse to publish it.
- **Fermat.** Clément-Samuel published the *Diophantus* commentary in **1670**, five years after his father's death.
- **Copernicus.** *De revolutionibus* was printed at Nuremberg in **1543** as he lay dying; his fellow clerics brought him the printed sheets, the only time he saw it as a book. ⚠️ *Caution: the popular reading — that he deliberately delayed publication to escape persecution — is disputed by historians and is often called the deathbed-publication myth. Cite the date, not the motive.*

**A dead man cannot be summoned, interrogated, or made to abjure on his knees.** He can be condemned, and his book can be put on the Index, and neither of those things hurts. The seventeenth century understood this perfectly well and used it.

So the fiction is not that Fermat used death to publish safely. **Everyone was doing that.** The fiction is only that he declined to wait for the real one.

⚠️ **One correction for the pitch document:** not the guillotine. That is **1792** — a hundred and twenty-seven years later, and a Revolutionary instrument. In 1665 the available ends are the axe or sword for a man of rank, the rope, or, for heresy, the fire. Say "lose his head" if you like the phrase; do not say guillotine in a room with a historian in it.

## ✍️ Invented

Short list. This is the whole of it.

1. **That the death was arranged.** The death is real, dated, and documented. The *faking* is the fiction — and the 1653 false report is what makes it plausible, because it establishes that Fermat had watched, at first hand, how easily and how completely Europe would believe he was gone.
2. **Marie.** And Agnès, and Duval, and the carter.
3. **The commission** to inspect French interests in Grão-Pará.
4. **Three ships of buffalo in 1666.** The largest invention, and it is a **relocation, not a fabrication** — the real animals arrive by shipwreck, off a French-bound vessel, and swim ashore. The series writes the version that would have to be true first, 224 years earlier.
5. **The voyage:** Bertrand, the crew, the reef, the abandonment.
6. **Aubin and the Le Mer as a named community.** The mixed French-Dutch-Portuguese-Tupinambá population is a documented consequence of two occupations; the surname and the fifty-one-year joke are ours.
7. **Fermat teaching the infinitely small under a tamarind**, and everything on Marajó.

## 🛑 The cost, which the series must not soften

You said 1665 is the year they declared themselves free, at enormous cost — the peril, and the family they left.

**The family is not an abstraction and it is fully documented.** Fermat had been married to Louise de Long for thirty-four years. Five adult children. And in the year of the death, **Clément-Samuel inherited his father's offices** — the son took up the bench his father was pretending to have died from.

So the fiction requires that Fermat let his wife of thirty-four years become a widow, and five living children become orphans, **deliberately, and permanently, and knowing exactly what he was doing**, and never sent word.

That is not a bittersweet detail. It is the worst thing anyone does in this story, and it is done by the protagonist, and there is no version where he is forgiven for it — least of all by himself.

And then the record delivers the ending no writer would dare invent:

> **Clément-Samuel spent the next five years assembling his dead father's papers, and in 1670 published the margin.**

The son gives the world the note about the space being too small to contain the proof — while, in this story, the father is four thousand leagues away under a tree, saying the whole thing out loud, at ordinary speaking volume, to thirty people who cannot read.

**The son published the silence. The father, elsewhere, was breaking it.** Neither ever knew.

That is the last scene of the series, and it costs nothing to stage, because it is what happened.
