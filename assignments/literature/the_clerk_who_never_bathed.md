# The Clerk Who Never Bathed With the Crew

Somewhere in the Atlantic, month three

A disguise, Fermat came to understand, was not a single decision made once at a dock in La Rochelle. It was a decision that had to be made again every single day, for months, against an enemy far more patient than any king's man: the ordinary, grinding sameness of shipboard life, which left no corner of a person unexamined for long.

The first difficulty was water. A ship's crew bathed rarely and carelessly, in full view of one another, and a clerk who never once joined them, who always found some pressing errand of the commissioner's at exactly the hour the men stripped down on deck, was the kind of oddity that sailors — who had nothing to do for months but notice things about each other — eventually noticed.

The second difficulty was her hands. Marie's disguise held up remarkably well from a distance: the cut hair, the borrowed coat, the deliberate slouch she had practiced in the mirror above the chandler's shop until it looked less like a performance and more like a habit. But her hands had spent twenty years being a court lady's hands, soft in a way that no clerk's hands ever were, and by the second month the ropes and the ledgers between them had roughened them, but not nearly enough, not fast enough, and Fermat found himself inventing reasons — an injury, a rash, a family superstition about gloves — faster than he was entirely comfortable inventing them.

The third difficulty, which he had genuinely failed to calculate for, arriving at it only with a kind of belated, mortified respect for a variable his own life had never required him to consider, was that a woman's body did not stop being a woman's body simply because a coat had been put over it. There were days, each month, when Marie disappeared entirely into the small cabin Fermat had commandeered "for commission papers requiring privacy," and did not emerge, and Fermat stood outside that door inventing commissioner's business for anyone who asked, and understood, with the same clear-eyed precision he brought to everything else, that this was the one part of the entire scheme he could not solve with arithmetic. He could only stand guard over it.

The man who noticed first was Bertrand, the ship's carpenter, a quiet sort who noticed everything and said very little, which Fermat had at first mistaken for stupidity and later came to recognize, with some alarm, as the opposite.

"Your clerk," Bertrand said to him one evening, not quite a question, "keeps unusual hours."

"My clerk," Fermat said, "keeps my hours, which are the hours of a man arranging a colonial commission from a berth too small for the paperwork it requires."

Bertrand said nothing further that night. But Fermat noticed, after that, how Bertrand's eyes followed the boy across the deck — not with suspicion exactly, not yet, but with the specific attention of a man doing sums in his head and not yet certain of the total.

Others followed. A sailor remarked, within Fermat's hearing but not quite to him, that the commissioner's clerk had a voice too gentle for the work he claimed to do. The cook, who fed the crew twice daily from a single vantage point that saw everyone eat or fail to eat, noted that the boy ate like someone raised at a table with linen on it, small bites, an economy of motion no clerk's upbringing had ever produced.

Fermat could not silence a whole crew's worth of small observations. He could only, with the same calculated patience he had once used to secure a royal commission from a minister who didn't want the posting, make the cost of speaking aloud higher than the cost of staying quiet — an extra ration here, a debt quietly forgiven there, a private word to Bertrand specifically that a commissioner grateful for a carpenter's discretion was a commissioner who remembered such things when postings and favors were handed out in the colony.

It worked, in the sense that no one said anything. It did not work in the sense that mattered most: by the fourth month, there was not a man on that ship who fully believed the clerk was a clerk, and a belief that goes unspoken for months does not disappear. It only waits, gathering a kind of quiet resentment at having been made to pretend, for the moment when pretending no longer serves any purpose at all — the moment, say, when a ship is breaking apart on a reef, and a crew already halfway convinced they had been lied to for four months finds it remarkably easy to row away and let the liars manage the consequences themselves.
