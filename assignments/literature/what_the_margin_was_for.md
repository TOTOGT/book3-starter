# What the Margin Was For

São Luís, the second year — under a tree, with an audience of children

He was quite clear about what would happen if it came out.

Not a fine. Not a stripping of office — there was no office; the office had been buried at sea in a weighted sheet with a carter inside it. A magistrate of the Parlement of Toulouse who had bribed a physician, used an unclaimed plague corpse, defrauded the crown of a colonial commission, and removed from Versailles a woman the king had informed of his intentions — that was not a matter that ended in a document.

He had spent thirty years handing down sentences. He knew what this one was, and he had known it before La Rochelle, and had gone anyway.

But it was not the hanging he thought about in the second year. That was simple, and simple things did not keep him awake.

What kept him awake was that the same discovery would kill Marie, and Agnès, and Duval — a physician of eleven years' loyalty who had found him a body — and possibly the ship's captain, and that every one of them had put themselves inside his arithmetic on the strength of his assurance that it would hold.

*I have made four people into terms in an equation*, he thought, *and I am the only one of them who enjoys that sort of thing.*

---

And yet.

There was a thing he had begun doing under the tamarind at the end of the north road, on the afternoons when it was too hot to be anywhere else, which had started as a way of not going mad and had become the most reckless act of his life.

He had begun to talk about the infinitely small.

---

He had never published it. He wanted that understood, in the account, by whoever eventually read it: he had *never published it.*

He had written letters. Letters were different — a letter is to one man, and a man can be trusted or not trusted, and a letter can be burned by either end. He had sent Mersenne things that would have been very difficult to explain in the wrong room, and Mersenne had known exactly how to handle them, and had died in fifty-eight, and after that Fermat had been rather more careful.

But he had never put it in a book, and he had never signed it, and where the argument was at its most dangerous he had done what he always did, which was to indicate the result and decline to write out the proof.

*There is not room in the margin.*

He had let two generations of correspondents believe that was a mathematician's vanity — the tease of a man who enjoyed being the only one who knew. He had never corrected them, because the correction was worse.

The truth was that a margin is small, and a small space is a place where a thing can be *mentioned* without being *taught*, and there is a very great deal of difference in law between a man who has mentioned something and a man who has taught it.

He had spent forty years mentioning.

---

The year was **1632**. He remembered it the way other men remembered a war.

He had been thirty-one. In that year the Society of Jesus, through its Revisors General, forbade the teaching of indivisibles in its colleges — the whole method, Cavalieri's method, the treatment of a line as though it were composed of points and an area as though it were composed of lines. Banned. Not disputed: banned, in the schools that trained nearly every educated man in Catholic Europe.

And in that same year Galileo was summoned to Rome.

Fermat had watched both from Toulouse, at thirty-one, with his own quadratures already in a drawer.

Galileo had been the most celebrated natural philosopher alive. He had had a pope who liked him. He had abjured on his knees in fifty-three, in a room, out loud, with the instruments in view, and had gone home to a house he was not permitted to leave and had died in it nine years later, blind, still under arrest.

*And that was what happened to a famous man with friends*, Fermat thought, *for saying where the earth stood.*

In fifty-one — he had been fifty — the Society had gone further and put indivisibles on the permanent list. Doctrines that could under no circumstances be taught or disseminated. Four of them touching directly on the infinitely small. Tacquet had written that the method made such war upon geometry that if it did not destroy geometry, it must itself be destroyed.

The objection was never really arithmetic. He had understood that early and had never said so aloud. The objection was that if a continuous thing is made of indivisible things, then the world is made of parts — and a world made of parts is a world that can be taken apart, and there were men whose entire authority rested on it not being that kind of world. The infinite belonged to God. It was, in the strict sense, an attribute. A man who handled it casually on paper was handling something that was not his.

Bruno had been burned for a version of that in the year sixteen hundred, and Fermat had never once heard a mathematician mention Bruno, and had always thought that silence was itself a datum.

---

So: a magistrate. In Toulouse. In a parlement that had burned people within living memory, in a city that took the Faith seriously and took *sedition* more seriously, holding an office that existed to enforce exactly the kind of order the infinitely small offended against.

He had been the wrong man, in the wrong city, holding the wrong ideas, with the wrong job.

And he had solved it the way he solved everything, which was to find the form of the problem that admitted an answer. He could not stop thinking it. He could not safely say it. Therefore he would think it completely and say it incompletely, and he would put the incomplete version where a small space made the incompleteness look like style.

He had made his own silence into a mannerism, and had let it become his reputation, and had let men write to him for forty years calling him modest, or difficult, or coy.

*Not room in the margin.*

There had been room. There had never once been room in Toulouse.

---

There was one further fact about São Luís, and he had established it in his first month, and it was the reason any of what followed was possible.

**There were no Jesuits.**

He had not believed it. He had asked three people, in three ways, over several weeks, because a Catholic colony without the Society was like a river without a bank — not unlikely, *structurally strange*, the sort of thing that indicates an error somewhere in one's assumptions.

There was no error. Five years before he arrived, on Pentecost Sunday of sixty-one, the residents of this town had gone to the Jesuit college of Nossa Senhora da Luz, taken the priests out of their cells, and held them prisoner in a private house. In September of that year every one of them, Vieira included, had been put aboard a ship called the *Sacramento* and sent to Lisbon.

Not by a king. Not by a bishop. By **the colonists**, who wanted Indians to enslave and had a priest telling them they could not.

Fermat took a long time with this.

He had spent his life inside the machinery — thirty years administering it, and forty avoiding one particular blade of it — and he had never once considered the possibility that the machinery could simply be *picked up and put on a boat* by ordinary men who had had enough. It was not a legal act. There was no instrument for it. It had simply happened, on a Sunday, in this street, and the men who did it were still here and would point out the house.

*They ban indivisibles in Coimbra*, he thought, *and in São Luís they carry the man who banned them down to the harbour.*

---

And the second fact, which arrived later and which he found he could not be glad about:

Vieira himself was, that very year, in a cell in Coimbra, being interrogated by the Holy Office on a charge of heresy.

Fermat received this in the way one receives news of an old adversary's illness. The Society had banned his mathematics; the Society's most formidable man in the New World had been thrown out of this town by slavers for defending Indians; and now the Inquisition had him. Everyone in the story was being crushed by the same apparatus, from different sides, and the apparatus had not been obliged to choose.

*It is not a party*, he thought. *I have spent forty years being careful of the Jesuits as though they were a party — as though there were men with opinions who could in principle be argued with. They are not the thing I was afraid of. They were only the nearest edge of it.*

---

He never once relaxed.

That was what Marie could not get out of him, and they were still arguing about it years later. The Society was gone; the college stood empty; there was no man within four hundred leagues who had been trained to recognise a banned doctrine or would have known what to do with one.

And every time he sat down under that tree he looked, first, at the road.

"They will come back," he told her.

"You do not know that."

"Marie. They have been thrown out of better places than this and have come back to every one of them." He said it without heat. "A parlement is a building and a king is a man, and both can be waited out. That is what the Society *is* — it is the institution that outlasts the men who expel it. In sixty-one this town put them on a ship. It felt, I have no doubt, extremely final to everyone present." He shrugged. "It will feel less final in thirty years, when the ones who did it are dead and the ones who benefit from it have grandchildren who do not remember why, and a ship arrives with four quiet men on it who are very good at schools."

He was right, of course, and did not live to see it, and had not expected to.

But it was why he never wrote anything down, and it was why the archive he eventually built was made of children.

---

The children under the tamarind could not read.

That was the first thing, and he had established it early and without any particular emotion: not one of them could read, in any language, and only two had ever seen a book, and there was no school within four hundred leagues that would have had them.

They were Le Mer, most of them. Some were not. They ranged from about six to about sixty and behaved, as far as he could ever determine, identically.

He drew in the dirt with a stick.

He had begun with a rope and a stake and the ordinary things a man can show anybody about a circle. He had gone on to the business of a curve and the space under it, because the boy who had asked him about the buffalo had wanted to know how much water a certain bend of the river held, and had not accepted *a great deal* as an answer, and had been correct not to.

And one afternoon, having drawn the bend and cut it into strips with the stick, and having watched a child of perhaps nine ask, entirely unprompted, *what if you made them thinner* — he sat back on his heels in the dirt with his heart going like a much younger man's.

Because the next thing was the thing. And there was no Society here. There was no parlement, no bishop with a college, no correspondent who might keep a letter, no drawer, no margin. There was an old man with a stick and thirty people the world had never once written down, none of whom could have testified in any court on earth as to what a doctrine even was.

"Thinner," he said. "Yes. Now. Suppose I ask you to make them so thin that they have no width at all — and yet there are so many of them that together they still fill the whole bend of the river. Both at once."

The nine-year-old looked at the dirt for a while.

"That's stupid," she said.

"It is completely stupid," Fermat agreed, "and it is also correct, and there are men in Rome who would very much prefer that neither of us had said it."

She did not ask who was in Rome. She had never heard of Rome. She asked whether it worked for the *other* bend as well, the one near the sandbar, and Fermat said that it did, and that this was in fact the entire point and had taken him thirty years to be sure of, and she said that thirty years seemed like a long time for something so stupid.

---

He walked back that evening past the fort with the French king's name on it and could not stop smiling, and Marie asked him what had happened, and he found he could not explain it in fewer than a thousand words and gave her four.

"I said it out loud."

She waited.

"The thing about the infinitely small. The one I have never written down anywhere that anyone could find. I said it out loud, in the open, in daylight, at ordinary speaking volume, to thirty people." He sat down heavily. "And nothing happened, Marie. Nothing at all happened. A child told me it was stupid."

"Was it stupid?"

"Extremely." He put his head back. "It is the best thing I have ever done, and the safest place in the world to say it turned out to be a country where nobody can read, and I have been sixty-four years finding it."

---

He never wrote it down there either. That was the strange part, and he examined it more than once and never entirely liked the answer.

The habit was forty years deep. A man who has spent his working life making sure the dangerous half of every thought stays in his head does not simply put it on paper because the bishop is four thousand leagues away. And there was a colder reason, which he admitted to himself only late: paper travels. Paper had been the one thing in his entire scheme he could not fully control — a manifest in Lisbon, a name in a book in Belém, a death notice in Toulouse. A written proof in São Luís would be an object, and objects go into holds, and holds go to Europe, and a proof in his hand was a signature.

So he taught it, and did not record it, and the whole of it — every step, complete, with the gaps he had never filled in for Mersenne or Pascal or anyone alive — went into perhaps forty people who could not write, most of whom did not know that the man teaching them had a surname, and none of whom had ever heard of Toulouse.

It was the least secure archive ever devised, and it could not be seized, subpoenaed, burned, indexed, or entered in any list of banned doctrines, because it was not anywhere.

*Nothing on the north road*, he thought.

---

*Note for the margin: in 1632 the Society of Jesus, through its Revisors General under Jacob Bidermann, forbade the teaching of indivisibles in its colleges; in the same year Galileo Galilei was summoned to Rome. He abjured in 1633 and remained under house arrest until his death in 1642. In 1651 the Society placed indivisibles among the doctrines permanently forbidden — four of the listed propositions bearing directly on the infinitely small. The effects were still being felt in Jesuit mathematics a century later.*

*Fermat published almost nothing in his lifetime. Historians have generally attributed this to the temperament of a gifted amateur who preferred correspondence, and there is a real scholarly literature asking why he stayed quiet on infinitesimals in particular. This book proposes the least flattering and most ordinary explanation available: that he was a magistrate in a city that knew how to burn people, and that the safest place for a dangerous idea is a space too small to hold the proof.*
