# The Children of the Sea

The north road, late

He asked Aubin about the name on the walk back, because it had been bothering him since the boy on the wall, and because he had reached the stage of tiredness where a man asks the questions he has been saving.

"They call you something," Fermat said. "In the town. I have heard it four times and I cannot make it out. It sounds French and it is not being said by anyone French."

"*Le Mer,*" Aubin said.

"That is not a name."

"No."

"It is not even correct. It should be *la* — the sea is—" He stopped, hearing himself, sixty-four years old, correcting the gender of an article in the only surname a whole people had.

Aubin was smiling at the road ahead.

"It has been said wrong for fifty years, senhor. By people who were not taught. It is ours now. If you fix it, it stops being ours and starts being French again, and we have been through that."

---

He explained it the way he explained everything, which was without any particular weight, as though it were an ordinary administrative fact and not the saddest thing Fermat had heard in a decade.

When a priest or a clerk or a sergeant with a book had needed to write one of them down — for a baptism, for a levy, for a body — the question came in the same order every time. Name. Father.

And there was no father to give. Not because nobody knew. In many cases everybody knew perfectly well, and could have pointed at a man, or at a grave, or at a horizon. But a name is not a fact about who begot you. A name is a claim on a family, in a country, backed by somebody willing to stand up and be counted for it, and there was nobody in France who was going to do that for a child in Maranhão, and the men who might have were either dead, or on a boat, or standing right there with no legal existence of their own to lend.

So the clerks wrote what they could see. *Of the sea.* The child came from the direction of the sea; the father came off the sea; the whole business arrived by sea and departed by sea and the sea was the only party to it that was still around.

"They meant it as *nothing*," Aubin said. "You understand. It was not a kindness and it was not cruelty. It was a man with a book who needed something in the second column."

---

Fermat walked a while without saying anything.

He was thinking about the men on the beach in the year fifteen — the ones too tired to face the water, who had looked at five months in a hold and a country where they owned nothing, and had turned around and stayed.

They had refused the sea. That was the single act of their lives that anyone would ever be able to point to. They had stood on that sand and declined, finally and permanently, to get back on the water.

And the sea had taken their children's names anyway.

---

"And this is why they cannot expel you," he said, at last. "I have had it backwards all afternoon. I thought the joke was that you are difficult to find. It is not that at all."

"No," Aubin agreed.

"A man can be expelled to somewhere. That is the whole mechanism — it is not a punishment, it is a *transfer*. You take a person out of one column and you enter him in another, and the second column has to exist, or the operation is not defined."

"Senhor, I understand about half of that."

"You understand all of it. You have been living inside it for fifty-one years." Fermat had stopped walking. "There is no France that will receive you. There is no Portugal that has offered. The forest is not a column in anyone's book. You are not a rebellion, you are not a garrison, you are not a parish. Every instrument they have is designed to move a man from one place to another place, and you—"

He was aware that his voice had gone strange, and did not much care.

"—*you have nowhere to be sent.* Not because you are hidden. Because there is no second column. They are not failing to catch you. They are failing to have anywhere to put you, which is a different failure entirely, and it is theirs, and it has protected you for half a century."

Aubin considered this.

"That is a great many words," he said, "for *we have no country.*"

"Yes," said Fermat. "It is. I have spent my life on that particular vice."

---

They walked on.

The thing he did not say — because he had known Aubin for one afternoon, and because he was not certain he could have got through the sentence — was that he had spent four days in this town believing himself to be the strange case. The dead man. The counterexample. The one entry in the world's books that had been made and then unmade on purpose, by arithmetic, at enormous cost.

He had done it deliberately, at sixty-four, with a physician and a carter's body and three days between a death and a departure in the same port.

And here was a whole people who had been born into it, who had never once been entered in anything, who had no France to be sent back to and no name that a clerk had not invented out of the weather —

— and who had, at some point in fifty-one years, stopped experiencing it as a wound and started using it as a wall.

*They are better at this than I am*, he thought, walking up the north road in the dark beside a man his own age who had never in his life owned a document.

*I have been dead for seven months and I am still checking to see whether anyone noticed.*
