# The Ceiling

The edge of the mangroves, and then the fort

The first sign of other people was smoke — a thin grey thread rising above the canopy, ordinary and domestic in a way that struck Fermat, after five months of storms and reefs and jaguars weighing their scent, as almost unbearably moving. Someone, somewhere ahead of them, was cooking fish over a fire, entirely unaware that two ruined Europeans and a very large, very calm water buffalo were about to walk into the middle of their afternoon.

It was children who found them first — three of them, barefoot, fishing spears forgotten in their hands, staring up at the buffalo and its passengers with an expression that held far more curiosity than fear. One of them said something in a language Fermat didn't recognize, quick and bright, and ran ahead through the trees before he could think of a way to answer.

"They've gone to tell someone," Marie said.

"I imagine they've gone to tell everyone," Fermat said. "I would, in their position. I cannot think of a single thing in my own life half as interesting as what they just saw."

The children's voices carried ahead of them through the trees, rising and multiplying, and somewhere in that relayed excitement Fermat understood, with the particular relief of a man finally permitted to stop calculating, that the worst of the journey was behind them. There was nowhere left to fall. Whatever came next — soldiers, questions, the long slow work of inventing a life — could not possibly be worse than the reef, or the crew, or the open sea.

It was exactly then, with the danger finally behind them and nothing left to keep her upright but will, that Marie's grip on him loosened, and she slid sideways off the buffalo's broad back before he could do more than lurch toward her.

He caught her before the mud did — his own arms shaking with an exhaustion he'd been refusing to acknowledge for hours, his legs barely steadier than hers — and hauled her, with a groan he made no effort to disguise, back across his own lap on the buffalo's back, her head falling against his soaked and filthy shirt.

"You are not permitted to die of relief," he told her, quite sternly, though she couldn't hear him. "I did not carry us through a reef and a curse and an army of blue-clawed demons to lose you to a *fainting spell*."

She did not answer, and the buffalo did not stop walking, and Fermat — dead tired in every part of himself, sixty-four years old and further from home than he had ever imagined being — found the only strength he had left was in his voice, and used it, murmuring to her in a low, steady mixture of Latin and old French that had nothing to do with what he was saying and everything to do with simply not stopping.

"We have hit the ceiling," he told her, over and over, counting it out against the rhythm of the buffalo's slow, unhurried steps. "There is nowhere left to fall. Do you hear me, Marie? The descent has a floor, and we have found it, and now there is only the climbing back up."

---

The gates of the fort, when they finally reached them, were already crowded with soldiers who had clearly been told to expect *something*, though nothing in their faces suggested they had believed it until they saw it for themselves: an enormous, mud-slicked, unbothered beast walking out of the mangroves at the exact center of the afternoon, carrying on its back an old man who looked like he had not slept in a year, cradling an unconscious young woman with a tenderness that belonged to no invading army anyone had ever been trained to fear.

A young officer took one look, crossed himself, and turned to shout something back into the fort that Fermat didn't understand but recognized instantly all the same, because it was the sound every group of frightened, curious men made in every language he had ever learned: *come and see this.*

He straightened his back as best his exhausted spine allowed, arranged his face into the calm, unhurried authority of thirty years spent commanding a courtroom's attention, and began, in careful, formal Portuguese, to explain that he was a shipwrecked merchant, that the lady was his charge, and that he would be very grateful indeed for a place to let her rest.

It was, he thought, the first entirely true thing he had said aloud in five months, if one did not count the merchant part, or the shipwreck, or very nearly everything else. But the gratitude, at least, was real. He held onto that, and let the rest be handled, for now, by the last reserves of a mind that had gotten them this far on arithmetic alone.
